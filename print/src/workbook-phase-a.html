How this workbook works

Left page tells you, right page is yours

Every step has an instruction page on the left and a working page on the right. Everything needed to fill the right-hand page is on the left-hand page facing it, so nothing has to be remembered from an earlier step or looked up elsewhere. Read the left page, then write on the right.

The work on each right-hand page is set out as numbered exercises. Do them in the order they are printed.

Moving forwards is checked, backwards is always free

Each step ends with a critical check: a short list the participant checks themselves. The step is not finished until every line can be ticked off. Going back, on the other hand, costs nothing and is expected. When a later step shows that an earlier one was wrong, go back, change the earlier work, and write the date and the reason in the strip at the foot of that step's page. Nothing is rubbed out. What was first thought is evidence of where the thinking moved.

Two colours of pen make that visible. Use one colour for everything written the first time, and a second colour for every revision. A page in one colour has either been right first time or has not been revisited, and both are worth noticing.

Nothing here is assessed by filling it in

A full page is not the point. A page with four actors on it and a good reason for the other eight being left out is a better answer than twelve names.

Where the words are defined

The glossary on page gives the short definition of every term this process uses precisely. The longer definition, with what the term excludes and why, is on the instruction page of the step that first uses the term.

The notes pages

The notes pages at the end, from page , are for anything that does not belong on a working page: questions that could not be answered, sources to find later, and disagreements the group did not settle. They are part of the work rather than a margin.

Summary and notes
Before you start

What systems thinking is

A way of explaining why a situation keeps producing the same result, by looking at how its parts are connected rather than at the parts one at a time.

The starting claim

Most stubborn problems are not caused by one thing going wrong. They are produced, over and over, by an arrangement that is working exactly as it is arranged. Meadows puts the claim at its strongest: a system, to a large extent, causes its own behaviour (Meadows, 2008, p. 2). An outside event may set the behaviour off, but the same event applied to a differently arranged system produces a different result.

That is why a problem can survive several sincere attempts to solve it. Each attempt acts on a part. The arrangement that produces the problem is left intact, and the arrangement reasserts itself.

What follows from the claim

  1. Structure produces behaviour. If a situation keeps returning to the same state, something in the arrangement is returning it. Finding that something is the work.
  2. The explanation is usually circular, not linear. A affects B, and B affects A. Causes and effects are not in separate columns, which is why a list of causes rarely explains a persistent problem.
  3. Effects arrive late. What is done now often shows up years later, and the delay is why people conclude that an intervention failed when it has simply not arrived yet.
  4. Where you draw the edge decides what you can explain. Draw it too tightly and the cause sits outside it; draw it too widely and nothing can be traced.
  5. Nobody sees the whole system. Everyone involved sees the part they stand in, and each of those views is reasonable from where that person stands. An analysis that treats one of them as simply wrong has given up on explaining the behaviour.

What it is not

It is not a way of proving what caused something. The output is an explanation good enough to act on, held together by evidence and open to being wrong, and the process keeps saying which parts are evidence and which parts are estimate.

It is also not a way of making a problem bigger. The discipline runs the other way: the edge is drawn deliberately, the number of parts is capped, and everything left out is left out with a reason.

What a system is

A system is a set of things, interconnected in such a way that they produce their own pattern of behaviour over time (Meadows, 2008, p. 2).

The three parts

A system has to have all three of these, and the order matters less than the fact that none of them can be missing (Meadows, 2008, p. 11).

PartWhat it means here
ElementsThe things the system is made of. In this process the elements that matter are the actors, and the quantities that rise and fall
InterconnectionsWhat links the elements: rules, money, information, who depends on whom. This is where most of the explanation turns out to be
A function or purposeWhat the arrangement produces, judged by what it actually produces rather than by what anyone says it is for

What is not a system

A collection without interconnections or a function is not a system. Meadows uses sand scattered on a road: add sand or take sand away and there is still just sand on the road. Take a player out of a football team, or a part out of a digestive system, and what remains is a different system (Meadows, 2008, pp. 11 and 12).

The test is useful in the other direction too. If nothing changes when a part is removed, that part is probably not inside the system being analysed.

Two things that follow, and that this workbook relies on

A system is more than the sum of its parts. Listing the parts and describing each one does not explain what the arrangement produces. The interconnections have to be drawn.

The function is read off what is produced, not off what is intended. A well-meant arrangement that reliably produces a result is, for the purposes of analysis, an arrangement that is set up to produce that result. This is uncomfortable and it is the most useful idea here.

Step 1 · before you write

Define the problem

To fix what is being analysed, what change is wanted, and who you are in relation to it, before any explaining starts.

The problem definition

The problem is written as one sentence in a fixed form. That sentence is called the problem definition throughout this workbook.

There is too much (or too little) of [thing] for [who], since [when], which matters because [consequence].

Then a short rationale saying how and why people are affected. Where the reason it matters is that something is unfair, the rationale says who is affected, compared with whom, and against which expectation. Two questions are worth answering in the rationale: what is at stake, and why do we care? What is driving us to think about this now?

This form does not fit every problem. Three other forms, and when each is needed, are on page .

Eight rules, read against the finished problem definition

  1. One problem per definition. If the sentence needs "and" to hold two things, there are two problems.
  2. Name a thing that can rise or fall. Not "the system is failing", which cannot rise or fall, but "the share of applications decided within the legal term", which can.
  3. No solution in the definition. An action, an instrument, or an organisation that should act is a potential solution: keep it for later, not in the sentence.
  4. No cause in the definition. "Because the ministry underfunds it" is an answer the analysis has to earn, not a premise to start from.
  5. No blame in the definition. Rewrite it so that someone who disagrees about the cause could still accept the sentence as a description.
  6. Bound it. Place, population, and period, so that the questions in Exercise 3 can be answered.
  7. A value can be the reason, not the fact. "This is unfair" is why it matters. The definition itself still needs what there is too much or too little of.
  8. Plain words. If a term would need explaining to someone outside the field, define it in the rationale or drop it.

On neutrality

The problem definition is not value neutral and does not pretend to be. Calling something too little or too much implies an expectation about what the level should be. The rule is that the expectation is named in the rationale rather than hidden in an adjective. "Too few inspections" hides the expectation. "Fewer inspections than the twice-yearly minimum the regulation sets" names it.

Other forms, positions, and size

The check before Exercise 6

Read the problem definition in Exercise 2 against two questions. Could someone who disagrees about the cause still accept the problem definition as a description? Could that person say what would have to change for the problem definition to stop being true? If either answer is no, the problem definition is carrying a cause, a blame, or a solution, and needs to be rewritten in Exercise 6. If both answers are yes, copy the first attempt across to Exercise 6 as it stands. Either way, the agreed problem definition is always written in Exercise 6, because it is the one every later step reads.

When too much and too little does not fit

The form on page is the default, not the only one. Take these in order, and choose one only with a line saying why the default did not fit. All of them carry the same five parts: the thing, the direction or the gap, who it affects, since when, and why it matters.

FormUse it when, and how to write it
MismatchThe quantity is right, but it is in the wrong place, at the wrong time, or with the wrong people. X exists, but not where (or when, or for whom) it is needed: it is at A while the need is at B.
Falling short of a standardThe thing exists and is counted, but fails a rule, a promise, or a quality that was set. X falls short of Y: the standard says A, the practice is B.
Not happened yetA risk, or a capacity that does not exist at all. There is no series to be too high or too low. If A continues, B becomes likely, and nothing currently prevents it.

The five positions, and what each one changes

Your position decides what the analysis ends in, so it is chosen once and carried through.

PositionWhat the analysis ends in
Analysing from outsideFindings. Your own influence is not part of the analysis
Acting for an organisation or a causeA target and an ask. Your own influence is part of the analysis
Advising someone who decidesOptions with a recommendation. The reach that counts is the client's, not yours
Deciding, or your organisation doesA decision. What is inside your own control is looked at first
Affected by thisA route through an organisation or a coalition, because the power available is mostly power with others

If the problem is too large, or too small

Exercise 3 is how you know. If you cannot say where the problem starts and stops, or cannot foresee about a dozen things that drive it, it is too large. If almost nothing drives it, it is too small.

Narrow by place, by population, by a single decision, or by period. Widen by asking why the situation persists, or by taking in the decision that sets the conditions for it. The size question in Exercise 3 is a judgement rather than a rule: it asks whether the problem is small enough to work on and still large enough to be worth explaining.

How others describe it, in Exercise 7

Find how the problem is currently described by those with power over it: a policy document, a press release, a minister's answer, an annual report, a minutes entry. Write their description in their words, not yours, and then write what that description takes for granted and what it leaves out. Give the source. One description is enough here; the others come later.

Step 2 · before you begin

Set the boundary and the perspectives

To decide what is inside the system and what is left out, and whose views count. This is where the case stops being a topic and becomes a system with edges.

Four words this step uses precisely

Actor. Someone who can act in this system or is acted upon by it. Any one of four tests admits an actor: 1 they do something that affects the problem, 2 they are affected by what the system produces, 3 they hold something others in the system need, or 4 they can start or stop a change. Two things are not actors: a category with no agency, such as the weather, the market, or society, which is a condition rather than an actor; and a named individual, unless the thing held belongs to that person rather than to their organisation.

Layer. A set of actors who work within the same scope of decision-making or implementation, meaning that what any of them decides applies to the same territory, population, or part of the organisation. Layers are ordered by how far a decision reaches, from the widest scope of decision to the narrowest, and by nothing else. They are not ranks, not importance, and not a chain of command. They belong to the system, not to you.

Actor type. What kind of actor this is, in this case. The list is written by the participant, at most five types, and as a guide no more than one type per three actors. An actor that seems to need two types usually means the type list is wrong rather than the actor.

Stakeholder is not used in this process. Everything inside the system is an actor.

Drawing the boundary

Almost everything is connected to almost everything else, so "is this related?" admits everything. The test is difference, not relevance: would including this change what the analysis concludes? A candidate goes inside if any one of three things is true. The candidate can increase or decrease the quantity named in the agreed problem definition. The candidate is on the receiving end of the excess or deficit of that quantity, meaning the candidate is part of who the problem is for. Or the candidate sets the rules the first two work under.

Everything left out is marked F fixed, O out of reach, or I irrelevant, with one line of reason. Fixed means it will not change during the period being analysed. Out of reach means it could change but nobody in this case can affect it. Irrelevant means including it would not change what the analysis concludes.

The boundary stays provisional. Everything marked F or O is written down rather than forgotten, because a thing assumed fixed that turns out to move is the commonest reason an intervention fails.

How many actors

About a dozen. Past roughly fifteen, either the granularity or the boundary has gone wrong: individual officials are being listed where the department that decides is the actor, or the boundary has taken in layers where nobody passes any of the three tests above. Check granularity first.

The resources an actor holds

Power, and why this step records resources instead

Power here is an actor's capacity to affect what the system does: what gets decided, what reaches the agenda, and what goes unquestioned. That capacity is hard to judge directly and easy to guess at. What can be recorded instead is concrete: the resources an actor holds that others in the system need. Recording the resources is this step's work; judging what kind of power they add up to comes later, once there is something to judge it against.

The seven resources

Seven kinds, enough to sort by and few enough to remember.

ResourceWhat it means
Formal authorityCan decide, or change a rule
MoneyHolds or can redirect the funding
Delivery capacityDoes the work on the ground
Information and expertiseKnows something the others need to know
AccessCan get to whoever decides
LegitimacyCan credibly speak for others
Public attentionCan make an issue visible, or keep it quiet

Two rules while you fill the table

  1. An actor that seems to need two layers is usually two actors, as a ministry's policy directorate and its inspectorate are two actors with different reach.
  2. An affected group that splits into halves wanting different things is two actors. A single entry that hides the split will not survive the rest of the analysis.

A third rule, about who gets left out

Include those the system acts on, not only those who act. Name the group by its position in the system ("households refused on income grounds since 2023").

The four columns before the resources

Columns 1 to 4 are the four admission tests from page : 1 affects the problem, 2 is affected by what the system produces, 3 holds something others need, 4 can start or stop a change. Tick every one that applies. An actor with no tick in any of the four does not belong in the table, and column 2 is the one to read back: if nothing is ticked down that column, the list has only the actors who act, and not those the system acts on.

Who decides, and who disagrees

Authority, responsibility, and accountability are three different things

TermThe question it answersWhere the answer usually is
AuthorityWho can take this decision, or change the rule the decision is made under?A statute, a mandate, a delegation, a budget line someone can redirect
ResponsibilityWho is tasked with carrying this decision out?A work programme, a contract, an implementation plan, a job description
AccountabilityWho is called to answer when what was decided goes wrong?An inspection regime, a reporting duty, an election, a complaints procedure

A layer can hold one without the others, and where the three sit apart that is the finding rather than a complication. It is often the finding: the layer that answers for a failure is frequently not the layer that could have prevented it.

Who the dependency question is asked for

Every actor's resources are recorded in Exercise 5. The second half, who needs each resource from them, is asked only for a subset. The subset is not a matter of your sense of importance: it is read off the allocation in Exercise 6, and completed while you fill Exercise 7. Any actor in a layer holding authority, responsibility, or accountability for the decision, and anyone who holds something those actors need. That is usually three to six actors. Every other actor keeps the ticks in Exercise 5 and nothing more, and that blank is a state rather than an omission.

How the actors see the problem

This is not the question from Step 1 asked twice. Step 1 recorded one description, the one used by those with power over the issue. Here you ask what each actor takes the problem to be, in that actor's own terms, and what that follows from. One line per actor, for the actors whose reading differs from one already written.

Mark each line evidenced (a document, a quoted statement, a meeting, an interview) or inferred. An inferred line is not guesswork: it names what it is read from. A line that cannot name what it is read from is a guess and stays out. Motive is always a guess, so record what an actor treats as the problem, never why that actor privately wants it.

At least two of the descriptions need to differ in a way that matters, meaning both cannot be acted on at once. Two wordings of the same description do not count. Nothing here asks whether a description is correct.

Who speaks for the groups the system acts on

For each group the system acts on, write the group and who currently speaks for that group on the line under Exercise 9, including "nobody".

Step 3 · before you draw

Describe behaviour over time

To show the pattern the system produces. This is the first step that looks at the case as something that has been happening rather than as something that is the case.

Behaviour means the system's, not anyone's conduct

It is the path a quantity has taken over time, and nothing about how people acted. Writing about officials being slow is answering a different question.

What to graph

What is measured is fixed. How you measure it is yours. The thing the graph is about is the one the agreed problem definition names: the thing there is too much or too little of. That does not change here, because changing it is how a case quietly changes subject, and nobody notices afterwards because everything that follows is consistent with whatever was graphed.

The measure is a separate choice, and it is yours. One thing can usually be counted several ways, and you take whichever count has a history you can draw: a waiting list, the number of people in emergency accommodation, and nights of shelter used are three measures of one thing. Write down which measure you are using, and why that one. If no measure of the named thing can be found at all, the thing named is not a quantity, which is a return to Step 1 rather than a fault in this step.

If the agreed problem definition used one of the other forms, graph the quantity that the definition compares against something. For a mismatch, graph the amount available in the place where the need is. For falling short of a standard, graph the measured level of the thing the standard is about. For not happened yet, there is no series to draw, so graph the nearest quantity that does have a history: the pressure that would produce the risk, or the capacity that would absorb it, and say why that is the nearest one available.

The period goes back to the last time the system changed

Back to the last major change (a reform, a crisis, a new instrument, a change of government with a policy attached), or roughly ten years, whichever is longer. Not for thoroughness: a period that starts after the last reform shows the system settling down, which looks like stability, and an explanation built for stability explains the wrong thing. Use intervals as coarse as the evidence allows. Yearly is usually enough, and monthly usually produces noise you will then try to explain.

This step tests Step 1

Step 1's second rule was to name a thing that can rise or fall. Here that claim is put to the test.

Shapes, evidence, and the system problem definition

The six shapes

Pick the one that describes your graph, and then describe why your graph is that shape. A shape you choose is a claim about the system. A shape you describe in your own words is a caption.

ShapeWhat the line does
Steady rise or fallMoves one way at roughly the same rate
AcceleratingMoves one way, faster and faster
Levelling offMoves one way, then flattens towards a limit
Up and downRepeatedly overshoots and comes back
Rise then collapseGrows, turns, and falls below where it started
FlatDoes not move, despite the pressure on it
Flat is a pattern, not a failure. A quantity that stays put while people push on it is being held there by something, and naming what holds it is the work that follows. Most problems that have survived several attempts are this shape.

Evidence is labelled, not ranked

A line drawn from documented accounts is a legitimate graph. What matters is that the reader can tell which kind each part of the line is. Where the line changes kind, mark the change.

KindWhat you write beside the line
MeasuredThe source and the years it covers
DocumentedThe documents, and what each one establishes about direction or timing

The two futures

Optional, and drawn on the same axes as the line. One dotted line for what you expect to happen if nothing changes, and one for what the desired change from Step 1 would look like if it happened. Neither is a prediction. Both are drawn before anything has been chosen, which is the point: they fix what better would look like as a shape, while nobody is yet attached to a particular way of getting there.

The critical checks

Checked by you. Nobody signs them off. A line that cannot be ticked off sends you back to the page named beside it, and going back is free.

Step 1 is complete when

  • The agreed problem definition states a situation, not a solution: what is wrong with something valued, with the reason it matters and for whom.
  • Your position is named.
  • The desired change is stated after the problem, not before it, in the same terms as the agreed problem definition.
  • The four questions in Exercise 3 are answered, and a known procedure would not solve the problem.
  • Potential solutions that occurred to you are in Exercise 8, not in the agreed problem definition.

Step 2 is complete when

  • What is excluded is written down, with a reason and a mark of F, O, or I.
  • The actors include those affected by the system but not involved in it.
  • Every actor sits in one layer and carries one type.
  • For every actor, the resources held are recorded. For the actors the dependency question applies to, who needs each resource from them is recorded as well.
  • Which layer can take the decision, which is tasked with carrying the decision out, and which answers for the result, are each named or recorded as nobody.
  • At least two descriptions of the problem are recorded, and they differ in a way that matters.
  • The system has been drawn on a large sheet, drawn again in this book, and its three sentences written.

Step 3 is complete when

  • The quantity graphed measures the thing named in the agreed problem definition, and a line says which measure it is and why that one.
  • The period reaches back to the last major change in the system, or ten years, and a line says which.
  • The line carries its evidence, with each part marked measured, documented, or estimated.
  • What has been tried is on the event strip, with the years.
  • The shape is named, with a line saying why the graph is that shape.
  • The system problem definition is written, and it is not the agreed problem definition reworded.
