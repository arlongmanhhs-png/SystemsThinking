# Step 1. Define the problem, the desired change, and your position

## What the step is for

To fix what the analysis is about and who is asking. There is no purpose field:
the participant's position, chosen here, frames every later step and decides what
Step 12 delivers. See `core/principles.md`, rule 3.

## What the participant does, in order

1. **States the problem** as an excess or a deficit of something valued, with the
   reason it matters, as a first attempt.
2. **Checks suitability**: is this the kind of problem the process is for?
3. **States the desired outcome** as the change wanted in the thing the first
   attempt names, not a way of getting there.
4. **Names their own position**: who they are in relation to this system.
5. **Reconfirms and neatens the problem definition**, writing it again as the
   agreed problem definition that every later step reads: rewritten where the
   first attempt carries a cause, a blame, or a solution, and copied across where
   it does not.

Solutions that surface at any point go to the parked list. The list blocks
nothing and is opened again in Step 7.

## The prompts

| Field | Prompt | Answer format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | What is there too much of, or too little of? For whom? Since when? Why does that matter? | "There is too much (or too little) X for Y, since Z, which matters because ..." |
| Desired change | If this changed for the better, what would be different? Say it in the same terms as the problem definition in Exercise 2, and do not say how. | "Less X" or "more X", with the scale or direction named if known: once for five years or more, and once for a nearer point one to five years out, each with its year |
| Position | What is your position in relation to this system? | One of the five positions below |
| Suitability and size | Has this been going on rather than happening once? Does it have a history you can describe? Have attempts to deal with it failed? Is it small enough to work on: can you say where it starts and stops, and foresee about a dozen things that drive it? | Yes or no to each, with one line of justification. A no warns and can be dismissed with a line |
| The disqualifier | Could a known procedure solve it if someone chose to? | Yes or no, with one line of justification. A yes means the problem is a decision nobody has taken rather than a system that keeps producing a result, and the topic is changed: it is a stop, not a warning |
| Parked ideas | Anything you already think should be done about it | A list, not used again until Step 7 |

## The form of the problem definition

> There is too much (or too little) **[thing]** for **[who]**, since **[when]**,
> which matters because **[consequence]**.

The problem definition is written twice. The first attempt is written in this
form, in five slots. The agreed problem definition is always written, in five
slots of its own (the thing, what is wrong with it, who it affects, since when,
and why it matters) in whichever of the four forms fits: rewritten where the
eight rules and the check below find a cause, a blame, or a solution, and copied
across from the first attempt as it stands where they find none. The first attempt is kept rather than crossed
out, so that both are visible, but **every later step reads the agreed problem
definition** and nothing reads the first attempt.

Then a short rationale (three lines on the sheet) saying how and why people are
affected. Where the reason it matters is that something is unfair, the rationale
says who is affected, compared with whom, and against which expectation.

Two questions from Bromell (2022) are printed on the prompt card for this field,
because they ask for exactly what the rationale is for: **what is at stake, and
why do we care? What is driving us to think about this now?**

### Eight rules

1. **One problem per statement.** If the problem definition needs "and" to hold
   two things, you have two problems. Choose the one you can work on and park
   the other.
2. **Name a thing that can rise or fall.** Not "the system is failing" but "the
   share of applications decided within the legal term".
3. **No solution in the statement.** If the words name an action, an instrument,
   or an organisation that should act, they belong in the parked list.
4. **No cause in the statement.** "Because the ministry underfunds it" is an
   answer the mapping has to earn, not a premise to start from.
5. **No blame in the statement.** If it says who is at fault, rewrite it so
   someone who disagrees about the cause could still accept it as a description.
6. **Bound it.** Place, population, and period, so that the size test can be
   answered.
7. **A value can be the reason, not the fact.** "This is unfair" is why it
   matters, and the rationale has to say why it is unfair: who is affected,
   compared with whom, and against which expectation. The statement itself still
   needs what there is too much or too little of.
8. **Plain words.** If a term would need explaining to someone outside the field,
   define it in the rationale or drop it.

### Worked examples

| Weak | Why | Rewritten |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Air pollution in Europe is a serious problem and the EU should act." | Unbounded, names a solution, no measurable thing | "There are too many days above the WHO particulate limit in Kraków, since the city's last clean air plan, which matters because it shortens healthy life expectancy for people living on the main roads." |
| "Young people are disengaged from politics because parties ignore them." | Carries its own cause and its own blame | "Turnout among 18 to 24 year olds in Portuguese municipal elections has been falling for a decade, which matters because councils set policy that affects them most directly." |
| "Housing is broken." | No thing, no direction, no bounds | "There are too few social rental homes for registered applicants in Dublin, which matters because households on the list wait years for an offer." |

The examples are deliberately from different countries. A set drawn from one
country teaches the method as a national speciality.

### When excess and deficit does not fit

It is the default form, not the only one. The alternatives are offered in order,
and one of them is chosen only with a line saying why the default did not fit.

| Family | Why the default form strains | The form to use instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mismatch | The quantity is right, but it is in the wrong place, at the wrong time, or with the wrong people. "Too little housing" is false nationally and true in one city | "X exists, but not where (or when, or for whom) it is needed: it is at A while the need is at B" |
| Falling short of a standard | The thing exists and is counted, but fails a rule, a promise, or a quality that was set. Decisions are made, but late; care is delivered, but unevenly | "X falls short of Y: the standard says A, the practice is B" |
| Something that has not happened yet | A risk, or a capacity that does not exist at all. There is no series to be too high or too low | "If A continues, B becomes likely, and nothing currently prevents it" |

All three carry the same five parts (the thing, the direction or the gap, who it
affects, since when, and why it matters), so the rest of Step 1 is unchanged and
Steps 3 and 4 still have something to work with. A contested problem, where the
disagreement is itself the problem, is not a fourth form: it is handled by the
representation question, which records the competing descriptions.

### On size

Too large and the mapping sprawls; too narrow and there is nothing to map.
Narrow by place, by population, by decision, or by period. Widen by going up one
layer, or by asking why the situation persists. The test is not a rule: it asks
whether the participant can say where the problem starts and stops, and whether
they can foresee about a dozen things that drive it.

### On neutrality

The statement is not value neutral, and it does not pretend to be. Calling
something too little or too much implies a standard. The rule is that the
standard is named in the rationale rather than hidden in an adjective.

### The check before the agreed problem definition

Just before the agreed problem definition is written, read the first attempt
against two questions. Could someone who disagrees about the cause still accept
the first attempt as a description? Could they say what would have to change for
the first attempt to stop being true? If either answer is no, the first attempt
carries a cause, a blame, or a solution, and is rewritten as the agreed problem
definition. If both answers are yes, the first attempt is
copied across as it stands. Either way the agreed problem definition is always
written, because it is the one every later step reads.

## How others describe it

One description by those with power over the issue is recorded, with a source.

1. **Where to look.** The words used by those with power over the issue, in their
   own documents: a strategy or policy paper, a budget line and what it is filed
   under, a debate contribution, a press release, the name the issue is given.
   Two or three sources is enough.
2. **What to determine.** What those words make the problem out to be. A useful
   set to choose from: a matter of individual behaviour, of capacity or
   resources, of coordination, of compliance and enforcement, of information, or
   of rights. The same situation described as "non-compliance" and as "a capacity
   shortfall" leads to different systems and different targets.
3. **What it assumes.** Who, by that description, has to change, and who
   therefore does not. This is the sentence that matters most later.
4. **What is missing.** Who or what the description leaves out, which is usually
   where the invisible power sits.
5. **How to write it.** Two sentences, evidenced: "X is currently described by Y
   as ..., which assumes ... (source)." Then: "That description leaves out ...".
   Where two powerful actors describe it differently, record both.

The participant is describing a description, not accusing anyone of bad faith,
and the wording shows that.

## The positions

Five to choose from. The default is analysing from outside: it is printed first,
nothing is pre-selected, and choosing anything else asks for one line saying on
whose behalf. The
prompt asks for a position rather than a role, because "role" reads as a role
within the student group rather than a position in the system.

| Position | Who this is | What it switches on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I act for an organisation or a cause | A campaigner, an NGO officer, a student writing a campaign for a chosen organisation | "Your own influence" applies in Step 7; Step 12 produces a target and an ask |
| I advise someone who decides | A civil servant or consultant writing for a minister, a board, a council | Step 7 judges the client's reach rather than the participant's; Step 12 produces options and a recommendation |
| I decide, or my organisation does | Someone holding authority over part of the system: a manager, an official with a mandate | Leverage inside their own control is available first; Step 12 produces a decision, not an ask |
| I am affected by this | A resident, a user of the service, a member of the group the problem falls on | Power in Step 2 is mostly power with others; Step 12 usually runs through an organisation or a coalition |
| I am analysing from outside | A researcher, an evaluator, a student with no client | "Your own influence" does not apply; Step 12 produces findings, not an ask |

## The critical check

The step is complete when:

- The agreed problem definition states a situation, not a solution: what is
  wrong with something valued, with the reason it matters and for whom.
- Your position is named.
- The desired change is stated after the problem, not before it, in the same
  terms as the agreed problem definition.
- The four questions in Exercise 3 are answered, and a known procedure would not
  solve the problem.
- Potential solutions that occurred to you are in Exercise 8, not in the agreed
  problem definition.

The criteria are self-checked. See `core/critical-checks.md`.

## What Step 1 does not do

It does not name variables. The size test asks only whether about a dozen things
that drive the problem are foreseeable, which is a question about scope. Variables are defined in Step 4.
