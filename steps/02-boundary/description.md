# Step 2. Set the boundary and the perspectives

## What the step is for

To decide what is inside the system and what is left out, and whose views count.
Step 2 is where the case stops being a topic and becomes a system with edges, and
Step 2 is where power enters: not as a separate exercise but as what each actor
holds and who needs it from them.

## What the participant does, in order

1. **Draws the situation sketch.** Everything known about the case onto one
   canvas, before anything is defined or sorted.
2. **Draws the boundary.** What is inside the system, and what is outside. Every
   exclusion carries a reason.
3. **Defines the layers** for this case: sets of actors working within the same
   scope of decision-making or implementation, ordered by how far a decision at
   that layer reaches.
4. **Defines the actor types** for this case, as a short closed list.
5. **Lists the actors**, each in one layer and with one type, including those
   affected by the system but not involved in it.
6. **Records what each actor holds, and who needs it from them.**
7. **Allocates authority, responsibility, and accountability** across the layers.
8. **Records** how the actors see the problem differently: at least two
   readings, each held by an actor on the actor list, that differ in a way that
   matters.
9. **Draws the system again** in the book, now that the case has layers, actors,
   and views, and writes the three sentences the drawing has to produce. The
   first sketch is left as drawn.

Items 2 to 4 are one movement rather than three exercises. The two short lists
are written first, layers and then types, so that each actor can be entered once
and tagged with one of each: one layer, and one actor type. That tagging is what
makes the later sorting possible: Step 7 groups candidate levers by type, and
Step 12 addresses the ask to a layer.

Two rules of thumb apply while the boundary, the layers, and the types are being
written. An actor that seems to need two layers is usually two actors, as a
ministry's policy directorate and its inspectorate are two actors with different
reach. An actor that seems to need two types usually means the type list is wrong
rather than the actor.

## The prompts

| Field | Prompt | Answer format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inside | What is part of this system? | A list, no cap |
| Outside | What is next to it but not part of it, and why did you leave it out? | A list, each marked fixed, out of reach, or irrelevant, with one line of reason |
| Layers | What scopes of decision are at work here? Name them, starting with the one whose decisions reach furthest | Three to five, named by the participant, with five as a cap, and two only with a line saying why the case has no third scope of decision. One line of reason for every layer |
| Actor types | What kinds of actor are there in this case? | A short closed list, written once and then chosen from |
| Actors | Who can act in this system, or is acted upon by it? | One line each, with a layer and a type |
| What they hold | Of the seven, what does this actor have? | Ticks, with one line where it is not obvious |
| Who needs it | Who needs that from them? | One line per resource, for the actors the dependency question applies to |
| Authority, responsibility, accountability | Which layer can take the decision, which is tasked with carrying the decision out, and which answers for the result? | One layer named for each, or "nobody", which is itself a finding |
| Competing descriptions | What does each actor take the problem to be, and what does that follow from? | One line per actor, in their terms, marked evidenced or inferred; at least two that differ in a way that matters |
| Situation sketch | Put the actors, the relations, and the tensions on the canvas | A drawing, and three sentences |

## Terms used in this step

Four words carry most of the weight, so each is defined and each is testable.

### Actor

Someone who can act in this system or is acted upon by it. Four tests, any one of
them answered yes puts someone on the actor list: they do something that affects
the problem; they are affected by what the system produces; they hold something
others in the system need; or they can start or stop a change. Excluded: anything
that cannot be named as a specific body, group, or person (the weather, the
market, society), which are conditions rather than actors, and named individuals
unless the thing being held is personally theirs rather than their
organisation's.

The four are tests, applied one candidate actor at a time, not a description of
what actors are like. **No to all four means this is not an actor here**, which
is nearly always one of two things: the candidate is a condition rather than an
agent (the weather, the market, society, none of which has a will or can be asked
to do anything), or the candidate sits outside the boundary, where it is already
recorded as an exclusion with its reason. Both answers are useful; neither is a
dead end.

**What the system produces**, in the second test, is not only the problem, though
the problem is where it starts: the thing there is too much or too little of, and
what that does to people. An actor passes the second test if they are on the
receiving end of what the system produces.

What the system produces is wider than the problem definition, and the difference
matters. A system hands out three things: the thing named in the problem
definition, its distribution (who gets it, who is refused, who pays, who is
exempt), and the side effects that come with it. The problem is one of those
outputs, the one the analysis has named. Read the second test as the problem
definition alone and the test admits only the people the problem falls on, which
leaves out everyone who does well out of the present arrangement, and those are
exactly the actors Step 8 needs.

So: someone who breathes the air, waits on the list, is refused on income
grounds, collects the charge, is served first, or is paid to deliver the service
all pass the second test. Someone who dislikes the policy but is touched by none
of what the system hands out does not. Where an actor is touched only by an
output that does not touch the problem, the boundary's own test prunes that actor,
so the two run in order: the second test admits, the boundary prunes.

On screen the four tests are tick boxes on the actor form, and at least one has
to be ticked before an actor can be saved. On paper the four tests are four small
boxes in the actor row. Making the four tests visible rather than implicit
matters because the second test is the one people skip, and the second test is
the one that catches those affected but not involved, which the critical check below
requires.

#### Where the actor list stops

Three rules, applied in this order.

1. **Nameable.** An actor is something you could address: write to it, sit across
   from it, point at it in a room. If a candidate cannot be named that
   specifically the candidate is a condition, and it belongs in the boundary's
   outside list. "The market", "society", and "the public" fail. "The municipal
   housing department", "the residents' association of Kolenkit", and "tenants on
   the waiting list in district 7" all pass, and the last shows that a group is an
   actor as long as the group itself can be named.
2. **Difference.** Would leaving that actor out change the analysis? The same
   test the boundary uses, in "Drawing the boundary" below: would including this
   change the answer? If nothing later in the process would read differently
   without that actor, they are scenery.
3. **About a dozen.** The same discipline as the dozen things that drive the
   problem in Step 1. Past roughly fifteen actors one of two things has gone
   wrong: the granularity, or the boundary. **Granularity:** individual
   officials are being listed where the department that decides is the actor.
   **Boundary:** the boundary has taken in layers where nobody passes any of the
   three tests in "Drawing the boundary" below. Check granularity first, since
   granularity is the commoner of the two and the cheaper to fix.

**Granularity: name the smallest unit that acts as one.** The department rather
than the ministry if the department decides; the ministry rather than the
government if the ministry decides. Split one body into two actors only where the
halves would answer the four tests differently, as a ministry does when its policy
directorate decides and its inspectorate enforces.

**An affected group is one actor** where its members would answer the four tests
the same way, and two or more groups where they would not: tenants on the waiting
list and tenants already housed hold different things and carry different
consequences.

"Different things" is too loose to act on, so the test is the four questions, one
at a time. In that example: the waiting-list group holds a claim on a queue and
experiences the deficit itself; the housed group holds a tenancy and experiences
the distribution instead, as rent and as the conditions attached to it.
Experience is the operative word: what separates two halves of a group is what
each of them meets of the system, not what they think of it. Both groups answer
yes to the second test, but for opposite reasons, and the second group has an
interest in the present arrangement continuing.

So the rule is: **split a group in two whenever the same test would be answered
yes for different reasons.** Those are the two halves that will want different
things in Step 8, and a single actor that hides them makes the consequences grid
in that step unwriteable.

**The last check, and it runs against the nameable rule.** Read the finished
list and ask who is missing who cannot speak here. The naming rule is the one
that quietly defeats the critical check, because the people a system falls on
hardest are often the ones with no organisation to be named by. Where that
happens, name them by their position in the system ("households refused on
income grounds since 2023") rather than leaving them out.

"Cannot speak here" is too vague to act on, so the five reasons are named,
because each has a different remedy:

- **No organisation to be named by.** Dispersed and unaffiliated, so no body
  carries their name and the nameable rule quietly drops them.
- **Not counted.** They do not appear in the data the system keeps: unregistered,
  refused at the door, informal, paid in cash.
- **No standing in the arena.** No seat, no consultation right, no vote.
  Non-residents, people in a neighbouring jurisdiction, and those who will be
  affected but are not yet, such as the next cohort or future tenants.
- **Speaking costs them.** They depend on the body that decides, so raising the
  problem risks their place in the queue, their permit, their tenancy, or their
  job. Silence here is a rational choice, not apathy.
- **Spoken for by someone whose interests differ.** An association that speaks
  for its members rather than for applicants is the standard case, and it is the
  hardest to see, because the seat at the table appears to be filled.

For each group the system acts on, name the group by its position in the system
("households refused on income grounds since 2023") and record who currently
speaks for the group, including "nobody". That entry is what Step 7 reads when it
asks where power is invisible, and what Step 13 reads when it works out whose
message has to be carried by someone else.

### Layer

A set of actors who work within the same scope of decision-making or
implementation, meaning that what any of them decides applies to the same
territory, population, or part of the organisation. Layers are ordered by **how
far a decision at that layer reaches**, from the layer whose decisions apply to
the most people to the layer whose decisions apply to the fewest, and by nothing
else. Layers are not ranks, not importance, not a chain of command, and never
relative to the participant.

In multi-level governance that order usually coincides with the familiar one,
running from the layer furthest from the people affected to the layer closest to
them: Union, country, province, municipality, neighbourhood. The two readings are
not the same test, though, and where the two readings disagree the disagreement
is a finding rather than a nuisance. A regulator can be close to the people
affected and still bind the whole country; a school board can be as near as it
gets and bind one building. Order by reach, and note any layer whose distance
from those affected does not match its reach, because that mismatch is often
where the problem sits.

### Actor type

What kind of thing an actor is in this case, from a list the participant writes
(for example: public authority, delivery body, representative organisation, firm,
affected group, knowledge body). The tool requires one type per actor and caps
the list at five types. No more than one type for every three actors is a
warning, not a cap: a dozen actors therefore means three or four types, and
fifteen means five. Sorting only does any work when the categories are
materially fewer than the things being sorted, and a type per actor is a second
list of actors under another name.

### Authority, responsibility, accountability

Three different things, and the gap between them is often where the problem
lives. Authority is who can decide or change the rule. Responsibility is who is
tasked with doing it. Accountability is who can be called to answer when it goes
wrong. A layer can hold one of the three without the others.

## Drawing the boundary

A boundary is not found in the world, it is chosen, so the boundary is written
down as a decision with its reasons, in the same way a method choice is, and the
boundary is revisited whenever a later step fails on it. Two people can draw
different boundaries around the same problem and both be defensible, as long as
each can say what theirs leaves out and why.

**The test is difference, not relevance.** Almost everything is related to almost
everything else, so "is this connected to the problem?" excludes nothing and is
the wrong question. The question is: **would including this actor, layer, or
factor change what the analysis concludes?**

**What goes inside.** An actor, a layer, or a factor goes inside if it passes one
of three tests, each read against the problem definition written in Step 1. In the
tests below "it" is that candidate, whichever of the three is being weighed:

1. It can increase or decrease the thing the problem definition says there is too
   much or too little of.
2. It is on the receiving end of that excess or deficit: it is part of who the
   problem is for.
3. It sets the rules the first two work under: what may be done to the thing in
   the problem definition, and what those on the receiving end may claim.

**What goes outside, with a reason.** Everything else, and each exclusion is
filed under one of three options:

- **Fixed.** It will not change during the period being analysed: the climate, a
  treaty, a demographic trend.
- **Out of reach.** It could change, but nobody in this case can affect it.
- **Irrelevant.** Including it would not change what the analysis concludes.
  Distrust this one. It is where a boundary usually goes wrong, and it is the
  only one of the three that never comes back.

Exclusions marked fixed and out of reach are not lost: both return in Step 10 as
the assumptions the theory of change rests on, and a fixed thing that turns out
to move is the commonest reason an intervention fails.

**Where to stop: three cut-offs.**

| Cut-off | The rule | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | What is inside should still come to about a dozen things that drive the problem, the same test Step 1 uses: can you foresee about a dozen things that drive it? | If it is not, the mapping in Step 5 will sprawl and explain nothing |
| Layers | Include a layer only if someone in that layer passes one of the three tests above: they can increase or decrease the thing named in the problem definition, they are on the receiving end of that excess or deficit, or they set the rules the first two work under | A layer whose actors do none of the three is context, not system |
| Time | Anything whose effect appears only beyond the period being analysed sits outside | If it matters anyway, it comes back in Step 5 as a delay rather than as a variable |

**The check before moving on.** Could someone who disagrees with your boundary
name what they would add, and say why adding it would change the conclusion of
the analysis? If they can, add that actor, layer, or factor. If nothing they name
changes what the analysis concludes, the boundary is defensible, which is as much
as a boundary can be.

**The boundary stays provisional.** If the mapping in Step 5 cannot explain the
pattern from Step 3, the boundary is the first suspect: something that moves the
level of the thing named in the problem definition was left outside. A mapping
that cannot explain the pattern is a named return, and the return reopens the
exclusions recorded in Step 2 rather than the whole step.

The underlying point is Meadows': boundaries are of our own making, and the
trouble starts when people forget they drew them (Meadows, 2008, ch. 4). The
test for what must be inside, that those affected but not involved are in the
analysis, comes from the boundary critique in the critical systems tradition
(Ulrich & Reynolds, 2010), and the boundary critique is what the critical check
below enforces.

## The actor types, and where the list comes from

The list is the participant's, and the list belongs to the case rather than to
the tool. The list is written once, in this step, before any actor is entered,
and the list then becomes the closed set that every actor is chosen from: a
dropdown on screen, a legend in the corner of the sheet on paper. Two cases by the
same participant can have different lists, and neither is wrong.

**A starter list is offered, not imposed.** Six to begin with, every one of which
can be renamed, removed, or replaced: public authority, delivery body,
representative organisation, firm, affected group, knowledge body. The tool never
adds a type silently, and the tool never fills one in.

**The form the list has to take.**

- Five types is the rule. Three to five is advice, not a floor, and nothing is
  refused for having fewer. No more than one type for every three actors is a
  warning rather than a rule, because a ratio is a judgement about granularity and
  not a question of completeness. Two
  actors per type is the point at which sorting starts to mean anything, so a dozen actors
  and a dozen types is a second list of actors under another name.
- Every actor carries exactly one type.
- A type that ends up holding one actor is usually a name rather than a type, and
  belongs merged into another type.
- A type that holds almost everything is not doing any work, and should be
  divided into more types.

**When an actor will not fit**, the list is wrong rather than the actor. That is
the signal to revise the type list, and the type list can be revised at any point
in the step. Revising the type list means re-reading the actor table to retype the
actors, and nothing else.

**What the types are for later.** The types are how Step 7 groups candidates (a
lever aimed at a regulator is not the same instrument as one aimed at an affected
group) and how Step 8 reads who gains and who loses. A list that cannot do those
two jobs is the wrong list, which is the test to apply if a list is hard to write.

## Allocating authority, responsibility, and accountability

Not a vocabulary exercise: the participant allocates each of the three to a
layer, and to an actor inside that layer where they can. Knowing the difference
is the easy half. Saying who holds each, for this problem, is what the later steps
run on.

| Term | The question it answers | Where you find the answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority | Who can take this decision, or change the rule the decision is made under? | Statute, a mandate, a delegation, a budget line someone can redirect |
| Responsibility | Who is tasked with carrying this decision out? | A work programme, a contract, an implementation plan, a job description |
| Accountability | Who is called to answer when what was decided goes wrong? | An inspection regime, a reporting duty, an election, a complaints procedure |

**What the participant does.** Take the decision the desired change depends on,
and name the layer that holds each of the three for that decision. "Nobody" is an
allowed answer and usually the most interesting one. If the case turns on more
than one decision, do this for at most two, and say which.

**The three patterns, and what each means.**

- **All three in one layer.** Concentrated, and rarer than it looks. Step 12 then
  has one target and the ask can be simple.
- **Authority in one layer, responsibility in another.** The common case in
  multi-level governance, and the reason for the sentence every student hears:
  the body that delivers says it cannot, the body that decides says delivery is
  not its job. An intervention aimed only at the responsible layer changes how
  the thing is done, never the rule under which it is done.
- **Accountability somewhere else, or nowhere.** If whoever answers for failure
  holds neither the decision nor the delivery, expect blame to move and
  correction to be slow. That is not a complication to tidy away: it is a
  balancing loop, and it belongs on the mapping in Step 5.

**What allocating authority, responsibility, and accountability informs.**

| Step | What the allocation is used for |
| --- | --- |
| 5 | A gap between the three usually appears as a delay, or as a loop that defeats correction |
| 7 | A lever can only be pulled where authority sits. A lever aimed at the responsible layer changes execution, not the rule |
| 8 | Accountability predicts who resists, because accountability names who carries the blame if the intervention fails |
| 12 | The target is the layer with authority over the chosen lever, and the ask has to be something that layer can actually do. Where accountability sits decides which arena creates pressure |

A case where all three sit in one layer and the problem persists anyway is worth
a second look at the boundary: the decision that matters may be outside the
boundary.

## How the actors see the problem

This is not the Step 1 question asked twice. Step 1 recorded **one** description,
the one used by those with power over the issue, with what that description
assumes and who it leaves out. That description was read off documents, and it
was about framing: whose words the problem currently comes in.

Step 2 asks something only answerable now that there is an actor list: **what
does each actor take the problem to be, and what does that follow from?** The
Step 1 description is not one of the entries: Step 2 records at least two
readings of its own, and one of them may be the powerful actors' reading again,
written against the actor who holds it and in that actor's terms.

**How to record what an actor takes the problem to be.** One line per actor, in
that actor's own terms, for the actors whose reading differs rather than for every
actor on the list: an actor who reads the problem the way one already recorded
does is marked as sharing that line instead of being given one of their own.
Three things anchor the answer:

- **What they hold and what they are responsible for.** The view usually follows
  the resource. An inspectorate sees a compliance problem, a treasury sees a cost
  problem, a residents' association sees a harm problem, and none of them is being
  obtuse.
- **What would count as success for them.** Often different from what would count
  as the desired change on the Step 1 sheet.
- **What they would have to change about themselves if the problem were described
  the other way.** That is the sentence that predicts resistance in Step 8.

**Rules.** Produce at least two views that differ in a way that matters, meaning
both cannot be acted on at once; two wordings of the same view do not count. Mark each
line **evidenced** (a document, a quoted statement, a meeting, an interview) or
**inferred**. An inferred line is not guesswork, and the difference is testable:
an inferred line is read off something already recorded, and it names both what it
is read from: what this actor holds, what they are responsible for, or what their
published role commits them to. A line that cannot name what it is read from is a
guess and stays out. Naming what would show the line wrong was asked for until
29 September 2026 and was dropped as too demanding to put to a participant, which
leaves "read from what?" carrying the whole weight of keeping an inference
honest. Motive is always a guess: record what an actor
treats as the problem, never why they privately want it. Five is plenty; past five
it becomes a survey rather than an analysis.

Nothing in this step asks whether a view is correct, or even coherent. An actor
can hold authority, legitimacy, and accountability and still read the problem in a
way that is badly informed, self-serving, or flatly at odds with what they say they
want. The requirement is only that the line says what the view follows from: what
they hold, what they answer for, or a commitment they made in public and cannot
now drop. "It follows from nothing recorded here" is also an answer, and an
interesting one, because a view with no visible source usually means something is
missing from the actor's entry rather than from the actor's reasoning. What is not
allowed is filing the view as a mistake to be corrected, because Step 6 has to
explain the system as it behaves, not as it would behave if everyone were
reasonable.

The conflicting pair is the useful part: two recorded views, held by two different
actors, that read the same problem incompatibly. Where two views cannot both be
acted on, that tension is usually where the leverage sits, and the tension is the
thing Step 6 has to account for.

**What the actors' descriptions inform.** Step 6, where the narrative has to
explain why each of these views is reasonable from where that actor sits rather
than treating those views as error. Step 8, where who gains and who loses is read
against what each actor takes the problem to be. Step 13, where a message per
audience starts from their description of the problem and not from yours.

## Choosing the scale

Layers are the participant's to define, and the tool constrains only their form:
ordered by scope, every actor must fit into one specific layer. The question that
sets the scale is where authority, responsibility, and accountability sit. If all
three sit inside one layer, the case is probably narrower than it looks and a
second layer is decoration. If the three sit in different layers, that spread is
the finding, and the layers that hold them are the ones that matter. Where those
three sit apart, record the spread plainly: it is a finding, not a complication.

An actor that looks as though it sits across two layers is the ordinary case
rather than the exception, and choosing one layer is part of the exercise: place
the actor in the layer whose scope of decision the actor actually works to. An
implementing body, one that carries out what another body decides, goes in the
layer whose decisions it carries out. Split the actor into two actors only where
both scopes genuinely apply, as with a ministry whose policy directorate decides
and whose inspectorate enforces. The discipline is the point, because an actor
allowed to float across layers makes the control markers in Step 5 unreadable.

## What an actor holds, asked as a dependency

Seven kinds, enough to sort by and few enough to remember: formal authority (can
decide, or change a rule), money, delivery capacity (does the work on the ground),
information and expertise, access (can get to whoever decides), legitimacy (can
credibly speak for others), and public attention.

The seven are Hathaway's (2016) mechanisms of influence under a plainer name. That
matters for what Step 2 is allowed to do: the concrete mechanism is recorded here,
and whether the mechanism counts as visible, hidden, or invisible power is read off
the record later, in Step 7. A participant who tries to classify an actor's power in
Step 2 is answering a question the evidence does not yet support.

For each one an actor holds, the participant also names **who needs it from
them**. That second half is what the later steps use. A resource on its own is an
inventory; a dependency is a relation, and a relation is something the mapping in
Step 5 can carry.

| What the actor holds | Used later in |
| --- | --- |
| Formal authority | Step 5 (control markers), Step 7 (who controls a lever), Step 12 (the target) |
| Money | Step 7 (whether the lever can be moved with funds), Step 8 (who pays) |
| Delivery capacity | Step 10 (whether the theory of change is deliverable at all) |
| Information and expertise | Step 6 (mental models), Step 13 (what an audience accepts as evidence) |
| Access | Step 12 (the arena, and the route to the target) |
| Legitimacy | Step 8 (who can credibly object), Step 13 (who carries the message) |
| Public attention | Step 7 (visible power), Step 12 (the timing of the ask) |

### Which actors the dependency question is asked for

Every actor's resources are recorded. The second half, who needs each resource from
them, is asked for a subset, because asking the dependency question of fifteen
actors produces a page nobody reads. "Matters" is not left to the participant's
sense of importance; "matters" is read off what the step records:

- Any actor in a layer that holds **authority, responsibility, or accountability**
  for the decision the desired change depends on, read off the allocation.
- Any actor who holds something one of those actors needs, found while the
  dependency question is answered: a line naming one of those actors as the one
  who needs a resource brings the actor holding that resource into the subset.

That subset is usually three to six actors, and the subset is checkable: a
participant can be asked to point at the allocation, or the dependency line, that
put each actor in the set.
Everyone else has their resources recorded and the dependency column left blank,
and blank is a state rather than an omission.

The dependency question is asked again, narrowly, in Step 7. Once a candidate
lever is chosen, the actors around that lever get the dependency question whether
or not they were in the first set, because by then the process knows which
dependencies it actually needs. That is the recheck rule doing its job: a narrow
second pass rather than a wide first one.

One plain question covers invisible power, and partial answers are accepted: **who
is not in this list, and why not?**

The question is deliberately one question and not a technique. Hathaway's four
techniques for finding invisible power (testing whether a recurrent description of
the problem actually fits the case, looking for a gap between what an actor says it
wants and what its decisions can achieve, tracing a description back through its
history, and reading the moments when the status quo is disturbed) need the mapping
and the narrative to work on, so they belong in Steps 6 and 7 rather than here.

## The situation sketch

The rich picture, kept but disciplined. On screen the sketch is a canvas where
actors, relations, and tensions are drawn, images placed, and links made, with
deliberately little room for text. On paper the sketch is drawn by hand, with
printed images if they help. The sketch comes first in the step, before anything
is defined or sorted: a mind dump, deliberately unstructured, and no part of the
sketch has to survive.

The second pass is a second drawing, not a return to the sketch (decided
2 October 2026). At the end of the step, once the case has layers, actors, and
views to arrange it by, the system is drawn again in the book, and the first
sketch is left as drawn, so that the first thinking stays visible. The second
drawing is when the three sentences are written, once, and those sentences are
what travel:

1. What this system produces, whether or not anyone intends it.
2. Where the disagreement is.
3. What surprised you when you drew it.

Without the three sentences the sketch feeds nothing, which was the objection to
the rich picture in the first place. With the three sentences the sketch feeds the
pattern in Step 3 and the first factors in Step 4.

## The critical check

The step is complete when:

- What is excluded is written down, with a reason and a mark of F, O, or I.
- The actors include those affected by the system but not involved in it.
- Every actor sits in one layer and carries one type.
- For every actor, the resources held are recorded. For the actors the dependency
  question applies to, who needs each resource from them is recorded as well.
- Which layer can take the decision, which is tasked with carrying the decision
  out, and which answers for the result, are each named or recorded as nobody.
- At least two descriptions of the problem are recorded, and they differ in a way
  that matters.
- The system has been drawn on a large sheet, drawn again in this book, and its
  three sentences written.

The criteria are self-checked. See `core/critical-checks.md`.

## What Step 2 does not do

Step 2 makes no causal claims: what affects what is Step 5's work, and writing
arrows here means the mapping is being guessed before the pattern in Step 3
exists. Step 2 names no variables. Step 2 ranks no actors by importance, because
importance without a lever is an opinion, and the ranking that matters comes in
Step 7.
