# Step 3. Describe behaviour over time

## What the step is for

To show the pattern the system produces, so that the mapping in Step 5 has
something to explain. Steps 1 and 2 say what the problem is and where its edges
are. Step 3 is the first step that looks at the case as something that has been
happening rather than as something that is the case, and it produces the one
thing every later step is answerable to: a named pattern, over a named period,
with the evidence behind it.

It is also the first honest test of Step 1. Step 1's second rule says to name a
thing that can rise or fall. Step 3 is where that claim is put to the test: if the line
cannot be drawn, the thing named cannot rise or fall, and Step 1 is wrong rather
than Step 3 being difficult.

## What the participant does, in order

1. **Chooses the measure.** What is measured is fixed by the problem definition:
   the thing there is too much or too little of. How it is measured is the
   participant's choice, and is recorded with one line saying why that measure.
2. **Chooses the period**, and says why that period rather than a shorter one.
3. **Draws the line**, at intervals as coarse as the evidence allows.
4. **Marks the events** underneath it: the reforms, the crises, the new
   instruments, the moments when someone tried to fix this.
5. **Records the evidence** behind the line, and labels any part of it that is an
   estimate.
6. **Names the shape** of the pattern, from a short closed list.
7. **Adds one further quantity**, where the problem definition rests on it.
8. **Draws the two futures**, optional: what happens if nothing changes, and what
   the Step 1 desired change would look like on the same axes.
9. **Writes the system problem definition**: one sentence naming the pattern that
   the mapping will have to explain.

Items 1 to 6 are the step. Items 7 and 8 are additions that some cases need and
others do not, and a case that stops after item 6 with a good line and a good
statement has done Step 3 properly.

## Terms used in this step

**Behaviour.** The system's, not anyone's conduct. It means the path a quantity
has taken over time, and nothing about how people acted. This has to be said in
as many words on the page, because "behaviour" reads as conduct to a second-year
student, and a participant who reads it that way writes about officials being
slow rather than about a line going down.

**The graph.** One quantity on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal, and a
line. It is not a chart of several things compared, it is not a bar chart of
categories, and it is not a picture of a process. One quantity, one line, per
graph.

**Pattern shape.** The name for what the line does, chosen from a closed list
rather than described freely. Naming the shape is what turns a drawing into
something the mapping is answerable to, because each shape implies a different
kind of structure underneath it, and Step 5 has to produce that structure.

**The system problem definition.** One sentence naming the pattern, which is a
different sentence from the Step 1 problem definition and does not replace it.
Step 1 says what is wrong. Step 3 says what the system keeps doing.

## What is graphed, and over what period

**What is measured is given. How it is measured is chosen.** Settled on
29 September 2026. The thing the graph is about is the one the problem definition
names, and it
does not change here: letting the participant pick a different thing is how a case
quietly changes subject between Step 1 and Step 5, and the change is hard to see
afterwards because every later step is consistent with whatever was graphed.

The measure is a separate choice and it belongs to the participant. One thing can
usually be counted several ways, and the one to take is whichever count has a
history that can be drawn. A waiting list, the number of people in emergency
accommodation, and nights of shelter used are three measures of one thing. The
measure chosen is recorded with one line saying why that one. A named thing for
which no measure can be found at all is not a quantity, which is the first return
in the table below rather than a fault in this step.

Where the problem definition used one of the alternative forms, the
quantity graphed is the one on the near side of the gap: for a mismatch, the amount
in the place where the need is; for falling short of a standard, the measured
level of the thing the standard is about; for something that has not happened
yet, there is no series to draw, and that case takes the route below.

**The period goes back to the last time the system changed.** Back to the last
major change (a reform, a crisis, a new instrument, a change of government with a
policy attached), or roughly ten years for a policy issue, whichever is longer.
The reason is not thoroughness: a period that starts after the last reform shows
the system settling down, which looks like stability, and a mapping built to
explain stability explains the wrong thing. The participant writes one line
saying which change the period starts at, or that it starts ten years back
because nothing changed.

**Intervals as coarse as the evidence allows.** Yearly is usually enough, and
monthly usually produces noise that the participant then tries to explain. The
test is whether a finer interval would change the shape. If it would not, it is
decoration.

## What counts as evidence, including when there is no series

Three kinds are accepted, and the rule is that the kind is labelled rather than
that the strongest kind is required.

| Kind | What it is | What the participant writes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measured | A published series: a statistics office, Eurostat, an inspectorate's annual figures, an organisation's own records | The source and the years covered |
| Documented | No series, but accounts that say which way the thing moved and roughly when: evaluations, audit reports, parliamentary answers, press coverage, minutes | The documents, and what each one establishes about direction or timing |
| Estimated | Neither, so the line is the participant's own reading | What the estimate rests on, and the words "this line is an estimate" printed beside it |

A line drawn from documented accounts is a legitimate graph, and it is what the
systems literature means by a reference mode: the shape is the claim, not the
numbers. What is not allowed is an unlabelled mixture, where a measured section
runs into an estimated one and the reader cannot tell which is which. Where the
line changes kind, the change is marked.

**Where there is no series and no account**, which is the ordinary case for a
risk stated in the Step 1 "not happened yet" form, Step 3 graphs the nearest
thing that does have a history: the pressure that would produce the risk, or the
capacity that would absorb it, with one line saying why that is the nearest
available quantity. A case that cannot produce even that is a case with no
history, and a case with no history is one the mapping has nothing to explain,
which is a finding worth surfacing at the Step 3 critical check rather than in
Step 5.

## The event strip

Under the line, a strip marking what happened and when: reforms, crises, new
instruments, court rulings, changes of government with a policy attached, and
previous attempts to fix this problem. It is required, not decoration, and it
does three things. It shows whether the line moved when someone acted, which is
the cheapest evidence about what the system responds to. It gives Step 5 its
first candidates for delays, because an event followed by a change two years
later is a delay. And it is the record of what has already been tried, which Step
7 reads before proposing anything, since a lever that was pulled in 2019 and did
nothing is not a new idea.

## Naming the shape

One shape from a closed list, with one line saying why the graph is that shape.
Asking why this shape rather than the next most likely was deliberately left out:
it assumes a participant can rank the shapes they did not choose, and ranking
them is not part of the work.

| Shape | What the line does | What the mapping will have to contain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steady rise or fall | Moves one way at roughly the same rate | Something that keeps adding or draining, with nothing yet pushing back |
| Accelerating | Moves one way, faster and faster | A reinforcing loop |
| Levelling off | Moves one way, then flattens towards a limit | A balancing loop, and a limit worth naming |
| Up and down | Repeatedly overshoots and comes back | A balancing loop with a delay in it |
| Rise then collapse | Grows, turns, and falls below where it started | A reinforcing loop running into something it erodes |
| Flat | Does not move, despite the pressure on it | Something holding it there, which is usually the interesting finding |

**Flat is a pattern.** A quantity that stays put while people push on it is held
there by something, and naming what holds it is exactly what Step 5 is for. Most
policy problems that have resisted several attempts are this shape.

**The shape is provisional.** If Step 5 cannot produce a structure that would
make this shape, either the shape was misread or the boundary is wrong, and that
is a named return to Step 3 and then to Step 2.

## The system problem definition

> Since **[when]**, **[the quantity]** has **[shape]**, and **[what has been
> tried]** has not changed it.

It is not the problem definition reworded. Step 1 says what is wrong and for whom,
which is a judgement about a situation. Step 3 says what the system keeps doing,
which is a claim about a pattern that someone could check. Both sentences appear
together on the page, because a participant who writes the same sentence twice
has not understood what Step 3 adds.

The system problem definition is what Step 5 has to explain, what Step 6's
narrative has to account for, what Step 9's hypothesis says will change, and
what Step 11 measures.

## The two futures

Optional, and on the same axes as the line. One dotted line for what happens if
nothing changes, and one for what the Step 1 desired change would look like if it
happened. Neither is a prediction, and both are drawn before any intervention has
been chosen, which is the point: they fix what "better" would look like as a
shape, at a time when nobody is yet attached to a particular way of getting
there. They are optional because a case with a flat line and a thin evidence base
can produce two futures that are pure invention.

## What Step 3 sends back

| What happens | What it means | Return to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The line cannot be drawn because the thing does not move | Step 1's rule 2 was broken: what was named is a condition or a judgement rather than a quantity | Step 1, rename the thing |
| The line can be drawn but it is not the problem | The problem definition named a proxy | Step 1, name the quantity the rationale was about |
| The period needs to reach outside the boundary | The last major change sits in a layer Step 2 left out | Step 2, reopen the exclusions |

This is the commonest return in the process, and it is cheap here and expensive
later.

## The critical check

The step is complete when:

- The quantity graphed measures the thing named in the agreed problem
  definition, and a line says which measure it is and why that one.
- The period reaches back to the last major change in the system, or ten years,
  and a line says which.
- The line carries its evidence, with each part marked measured, documented, or
  estimated.
- What has been tried is on the event strip, with the years.
- The shape is named, with a line saying why the graph is that shape.
- The system problem definition is written, and it is not the agreed problem
  definition reworded.

The criteria are self-checked. See `core/critical-checks.md`.

## What carries forward

| Output | Goes to | Used as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The graph and its shape | Steps 5, 6 | What the mapping has to produce, and what the narrative has to account for |
| The system problem definition | Steps 5, 6, 9, 11 | The pattern Step 9 says will change, and Step 11 measures |
| The event strip | Steps 5, 7, 10 | First candidates for delays, what has already been tried, and what the plan is assuming about timing |
| The evidence and its kind | Steps 10, 11 | How much weight the plan can put on the baseline, and what an indicator can realistically be |
| The two futures, where drawn | Steps 9, 11 | The shape the hypothesis claims, and the gap the early signals read |

## What Step 3 does not do

It explains nothing: why the line has that shape is Step 5's work. It names no
factors, which is Step 4. It does not judge whether the pattern is good or bad,
because Step 1 already did. And it does not choose what to do about the pattern,
however obvious that seems once the event strip shows what has failed before.

## Settled, 29 September 2026

The four questions that stood open on this step are answered. What is measured is
given and how it is measured is chosen, with the measure recorded and justified in
one line. Six pattern shapes is the closed list. The two futures are optional. And
evidence stays labelled rather than ranked, with no measured series required,
because requiring one would rule out most of the cases this process is for.

Nothing on Step 3 is open.
