## Read and checked against the text

- **Bardach, E. (2012). *A practical guide for policy analysis: The eightfold
  path to more effective problem solving* (4th ed.). CQ Press.** Read on 28
  September 2026 from the copy in the literature folder, which replaces the
  teaching summary the form was previously checked against. Deficit and excess
  is step one, "Define the Problem", pages 1 and 2, under the heading "Think of
  Deficit and Excess". Bardach puts it as a semantic tip that often, though not
  always, helps, rather than as the required form, and he names the two cases
  where it does not help: a problem that is already a well-structured choice
  between decisions, and a challenge whose task is to invent a way of meeting an
  objective that has already been set. He advises putting the word "too" in the
  definition, and notes that a problem worth attention may be in prospect rather
  than present, which is the ground for the risk form. Page 5, "Quantify If
  Possible": an assertion of deficit or excess should carry a magnitude, and a
  guess with a stated range is acceptable where a figure is not. Pages 9 and 10:
  a definition that names the missing thing smuggles a solution into the
  problem, so "too little shelter for homeless families" becomes "too many
  families are homeless". Pages 2 and 3, "Make the Definition Evaluative": a
  problem definition rests on an evaluative judgement, which Bardach treats as a
  property of problem definition rather than a fault to be corrected.
  Not verified: whether the sixth edition (Bardach & Patashnik, 2019) keeps this
  wording. The literature folder holds the fourth edition, so the fourth edition
  is what the step files cite until the sixth is seen.
  Decided 2 October 2026: the fourth edition stays the cited edition, because the
  fourth edition is the one that was read. Which edition is on Ashley's shelf no
  longer matters to the citation, and the question of the shelf copy is closed.
- **Hathaway, T. (2016). Lukes reloaded: An actor-centred three-dimensional
  power framework. *Politics, 36*(2), 118-130.
  https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9256.12099** Read on 28 September 2026 from the
  copy in the literature folder. Hathaway builds an empirically usable framework
  on Lukes and on Gaventa's powercube, and the reason it suits this process is
  that the framework is actor-centred: power is traced through the mechanisms of
  influence a named actor holds, not through an abstract face of power. The
  sequence he sets out is the one Step 2 already follows. First isolate the
  concrete mechanisms (for visible power, decision making and the control of
  resources; for hidden power, backdoor dealing and institutional organisation;
  for invisible power, the making and use of discourse, and the discourse that
  empowers the actor in the first place). Only then classify them, and treat the
  classification as provisional, since a mechanism can move from hidden to
  invisible as an idea settles into accepted truth. He distinguishes direct
  (agential) from indirect (structural) mechanisms, and warns against reading
  luck as power: an actor whose interests happen to be realised is not thereby
  powerful. For the invisible power question he gives four techniques: test
  whether a recurrent discourse actually fits the policy area; look for a gap
  between what actors say they want, what they decide, and what those decisions
  can achieve; trace an influential discourse back through its history; and read
  the moments when the status quo is disturbed, because those moments test the
  ideas that hold it up.
  Decided 28 September 2026: Hathaway is the power frame for the process. Lukes
  and Gaventa reach the process through him rather than being cited directly, the
  powercube's vocabulary of form, space, and level is not carried over, and
  VeneKlasen and Miller stay only where the advocacy steps need a word for what an
  actor's power is for.

- **Bromell (2022, second edition).** Read on 28 September 2026. He does **not**
  use "excess" or "deficit" for problem definition anywhere in the book; the
  only two occurrences of either word are unrelated. He reproduces Bardach and
  Patashnik's eightfold path as Table 1.1 (p. 9), whose first step is "Define
  the problem", and quotes Bardach and Patashnik (2019, p. 2) on which private
  troubles warrant definition as public problems. His own framing, in the public
  value chapter, is "Define the Problem/Risk/Opportunity. What is at stake, and
  why do we care? What is driving us to think about this now?", which is a
  candidate citation for the Step 1 rationale rather than for the problem
  definition form. Decided 28 September 2026: he is cited for the Step 1
  rationale prompt, and his two questions are printed on the prompt card. He is
  not cited for the problem definition form.
