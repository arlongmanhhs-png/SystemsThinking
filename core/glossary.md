# Glossary

These words are used precisely. They are not interchangeable with their everyday
synonyms, and participant-facing text uses them as defined here.

**Analysis.** What the participant is doing. Not "inquiry", which reads as
stilted, and not "investigation".

**Mapping.** The activity and its product in Step 5. Not "the map", which makes
it sound like a finished object.

**Problem.** An excess or a deficit of something valued, bounded in place,
population, and period, with the reason it matters. Three alternative forms exist
for cases the default does not fit (mismatch, falling short of a standard, and
something that has not happened yet): see `steps/01-framing/description.md`.

**Desired change.** What would be different, stated in the same terms as the
problem. Not a way of getting there. Where it reads better it is written in full
as the **desired outcome**, always in full and never as a bare "outcome".

**Outcome.** From Step 9 onwards, the result an intervention is meant to produce,
which is the standard theory of change sense and what Step 11 measures. The word
is never used on its own for anything else.

**End variable.** A variable at the end of a causal chain, as against a driver at
its start. Step 5 identifies drivers and end variables. This replaces "drivers and
outcomes", which used "outcome" for a position in a structure rather than a
result. Decided 28 September 2026.

**Purpose.** Retired on 28 September 2026. There is no purpose field: the
process is for policy and intervention design, and the participant's position
frames what the analysis delivers. See Position, and `core/principles.md`, rule 3.

**Position.** Where the participant stands in relation to the system, chosen from
five. The default is analysing from outside. It is deliberately not called a
role, which in a student project reads as a role within the group.

**Actor.** Someone who can act in this system or is acted upon by it. Four
tests, and any one of them answered yes puts someone on the actor list: they do
something that affects the problem; they are affected by what the system
produces; they hold something others in the system need; or they can start or
stop a change. No to all four means the candidate is not an actor here, which is
nearly always one of two things: the candidate is a condition rather than an
agent, or the candidate sits outside the boundary, where it is already recorded
as an exclusion with its reason. Also excluded: anything that cannot be named as
a specific body, group, or person (the weather, the market, society), and named
individuals unless the thing being held is personally theirs rather than their
organisation's.

**Stakeholder.** Not a synonym for actor. "Stakeholder" belongs to the advocacy
side, where the question is who holds a stake in the outcome of a campaign, and
"stakeholder" stays in the teaching there. Inside the system the question is a
different one, who acts and who is acted upon, so the tool asks for actors. Actor
and stakeholder are not two words for one thing: they answer different questions,
in different steps.

**Layer.** A set of actors who work within the same scope of decision-making or
implementation, meaning that what any of them decides applies to the same
territory, population, or part of the organisation: the workbook's wording,
adopted on 2 October 2026. Layers are ordered by how far a decision at that
layer reaches, from the layer whose decisions apply to the most people to the
layer whose decisions apply to the fewest, and by nothing else. Layers are not
ranks, not importance, not a chain of command, and never relative to the
participant. The participant defines the layers for their own case: three to
five of them, with five as a cap the tool enforces, and two only with a line
saying why the case has no third scope of decision.

**Actor type.** What kind of thing an actor is in this case, from a list the
participant writes (for example: public authority, delivery body, representative
organisation, firm, affected group, knowledge body). The tool requires one type
per actor and caps the list at five. Three to five is advice rather than a floor,
and no more than one type for every three actors is a warning rather than a cap:
decided 29 September 2026, because a ratio is a judgement about granularity and
not a question of completeness.

**Authority.** Who can take a decision, or change the rule the decision is made
under. Found in a statute, a mandate, a delegation, or a budget line someone can
redirect.

**Responsibility.** Who is tasked with carrying a decision out. Found in a work
programme, a contract, an implementation plan, or a job description.

**Accountability.** Who is called to answer when what was decided goes wrong.
Found in an inspection regime, a reporting duty, an election, or a complaints
procedure. A layer can hold authority, responsibility, or accountability without
holding the other two, and the gap between the three is often where the problem
lives.

**Dependency question.** The second half of what an actor holds: for each of the
seven resources an actor has, who needs that resource from them. A resource on its
own is an inventory; a dependency is a relation, and a relation is something the
mapping in Step 5 can carry. The dependency question is asked for a subset of
actors rather than left to the participant's sense of importance: any actor in a
layer holding authority, responsibility, or accountability for the decision, read
off the allocation, and anyone found, while the dependency question is answered,
to hold something those actors need. The dependency question is asked again,
narrowly, in Step 7.

**Factor.** What is collected in Step 4, before it has been disciplined.

**Quantity.** What Step 3 graphs over time: the thing the problem definition
names, counted in whichever way has a history that can be drawn. What is measured
is given by Step 1; how it is measured is the participant's choice, recorded with
one line saying why that measure. A quantity is not a variable: a variable is a
node in the causal mapping and Step 1 has none yet, which is why the Step 1 size
test asks about "things that drive it" rather than about variables.

**Variable.** Something in the system that can be more or less, and whose level
can be observed or reasonably judged. A factor becomes a variable once it passes
the naming rules: a noun, observable, neutral, and able to rise or fall. An actor
is not a variable, and a judgement (such as "unfairness") is not a variable.

**Stock.** A variable that accumulates. **Flow.** A variable that adds to or
drains a stock. Stocks begin in Step 1 (the excess or deficit is a stock standing
too high or too low), are typed in Step 4, marked in Step 5, and drawn as a stock
and flow diagram only where one is needed, in Step 10.

**Level.** How much of a variable there is at a given moment. Never the "level"
of multi-level governance, which this process calls a **layer**. Where a sentence
could be read either way, name which is meant.

**Boundary.** What is inside the system and what is left out, stated with a
reason for the exclusions. Boundaries are chosen, not found (Meadows, 2008,
ch. 4). The test is difference, not relevance: would including this actor, layer,
or factor change what the analysis concludes? A candidate goes inside if it can
increase or decrease the thing named in the problem definition, is on the
receiving end of that excess or deficit, or sets the rules the first two work
under. Everything else is outside, marked fixed, out of reach, or irrelevant,
each exclusion carrying one line of reason.

**Critical check.** The last part of a step: the criteria the participant checks
their own work against. See `core/critical-checks.md`.

**Return trigger.** A named consequence of a criterion failing, saying which step
or which fields reopen.

**Parked idea.** A solution that surfaced before the process was ready for it.
Parked ideas are recorded at any step, block nothing, and are opened again at
Step 7.
